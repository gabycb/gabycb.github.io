## Intro
If you know me and have seen my calendar, then you know how ya girl is busy. I rarely take time to pause or stop and think. I know this is one of my flaws (thanks Brene Brown for helping me embrace my flaws, see her book [Dare to Lead](https://brenebrown.com/hubs/dare-to-lead/). So, I must force myself to do what does not come naturally, which is why I signed up for a course on ethics from Stanford, link to course [here](https://online.stanford.edu/courses/soe-xetech0001-ethics-technology-public-policy-practitioners). Only in a class would I stop and have the opportunity to think through things instead of jumping in right into the deep-end, which is how I usually take part in anything tech related (and almost always, ends up being a big mistake, huge). I want to share two main takeaways from this course relevant for my fellow tech practitioners in this age of AI: moral agency for human responsibility, and the messiness in trying to balance the harms and benefits of technology like generative AI. I plan to implement some easy and some hard lessons gleaned as part of these takeaways, and I’m sharing these so you can also benefit from the interactions I was lucky to have with the brilliant minds and inspiring leaders among the lecturers, professors, and fellow students in this course.

## Key Learnings 
The course is book-ended with Ursula Le Guin at the start and N.K. Jemisin at the end. So you already know it’s going to be spicy and fascinating. I highly recommend reading their short stories, Le Guin’s [The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas](https://shsdavisapes.pbworks.com/f/Omelas.pdf), from The Wind's Twelve Quarters, and Jemisin’s “The Ones Who Stay and Fight” (2018), published in Lightspeed Magazine (Jan 2020) at this [link](https://www.lightspeedmagazine.com/fiction/the-ones-who-stay-and-fight/), like we did in the course (yes, I’m giving you homework). The crux of these stories, scoped to my reflections in thinking about AI today, is to make the reader question when they are willing to act on behalf of their moral agency. At large, this means readers should also question how technologists and engineers (but really, anyone involved in the AI space) act on their moral agency and in what ways are they held accountable for the human responsibility behind their choices, or not held responsible and what that means for society at large. 

For me, in my daily job in AI, this materialized in questioning how much responsibility I transfer to AI and agentic automations. Obviously by working at Microsoft, I get to test the latest and greatest, the frontier of development in technology, but I rarely pause to think if I _should_. I may be absolving my own responsibilities and essentially giving up my agency in ways I don’t fully understand by relying on AI more and more. This ethics course helped me question this and take time to pause and reflect on what this means for my moral agency (almost reminding me I have moral agency, to be honest). I can be more purposeful and deliberate. And what does this mean for human responsibility, in the larger industry? This course reminded me that humans, real people, are behind every decision in building these AI systems and how they are deployed. Greater transparency and acknowledgement of the market forces and incentives behind human choices is possible, and rather necessary when it comes to the often falsely-opaque impact and harms of AI on people, especially on people who do not have a say in the development of this technology.

I will practically apply these lessons and insights by questioning the words I see and use when talking about AI. Instead of treating this technology as outside human control or its ‘rogue’ abilities, I will remember there are real human choices that surface and carry through in everything about AI. Similarly, I have the agency to not use technologies like machine learning and AI. One of the most relevant industry examples in this being warranted was shared by Professor Mehran Sahami, Stanford profile [here](https://profiles.stanford.edu/mehran-sahami). He talked about a case study where Amazon conducted an audit of an algorithm used in recruiting decisions and when bias against women was found to be impossible to remove, the company decided to no longer use that algorithm for automations that might have saved time. See Reuters article covering this [here](https://www.reuters.com/article/world/insight-amazon-scraps-secret-ai-recruiting-tool-that-showed-bias-against-women-idUSKCN1MK0AG/). The larger course lesson was on the complexities of defining fairness, but the practical lesson for me as a practitioner was that you can just choose to not use an algorithm or technology, and that's valid and possible through auditing. 

The second takeaway from this course for me was a greater depth of understanding of the difficulties in balancing the benefits and harms of technology, both at the individual level and at a society level. For example with audits, even though there is agreement that they are necessary and should have greater implementation, there’s still unknowns and disagreements in terms of who exactly should be doing the audit, what metrics should be used to compare results of audits, and what should be the outcome of audits in terms of liability or corporate responsibilities. I’m still learning about other ethical concerns of AI covered in the course including algorithmic bias, accountability gaps, tensions with privacy and individual rights, and child safety and protections. Beyond these, I also had not thought about the diffusion of these and other harms at a broader scale and the increasing levels of complexity when you expand to include other communities not typically considered, like the Global South. Governance and policy are difficult to do at the state level let alone in the context of national or international standards, questions arise of democratic control and distribution of power, and there are so many unknowns about the future of work.

## Application
With that said, among the many unknowns and unpredictability in this space, this course also introduced me to organizations that are critically and intelligently thinking through these issues for possible solutions. At the end of this write up, I include a list of some of these and key links. Practically, I will apply what I learned from these organizations by engaging in more conversations outside my comfort zone and known network. For example, I never heard of [Empatia Lab](https://empatialab.org/index.html) before, which promotes ethical AI and research in Latin America, but I now know I can reach out to real people at that organization and I can learn about their experiences. Maybe best of all, this course helped me meet new people among my classmates and cohort leaders and instructors, and through our woes and laughs about the AI world we’re in, connect with them beyond a surface level. It’s easy to forget fellow humans are taking actions to chart a different path for AI, and this course helped me learn from exactly those people.

## Conclusion
This is not a comprehensive nor cohesive reflection of my journey in learning about ethics and technology. It’s a start, an exciting one that is now brimming with possibilities and imagination I did not have before this course. Like N.K. Jemisin says, “let’s get to work.”
If you have thoughts, feedback, or other advice on how I can keep learning or if you want to add any comments or notes, please contribute via github entry below. Comments can even be anonymous (note: all submissions are reviewed by me before publishing on this page). This is a start to a discussion with my fellow humans working with AI who are also wondering about moral agency and responsibility.
